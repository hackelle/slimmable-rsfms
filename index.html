How Much of a Model Do We Need?
Redundancy and Slimmability in Remote Sensing Foundation Models

Cut the width of a pretrained remote sensing foundation model to a few percent of its compute, with no retraining, and it still keeps most of its accuracy. Computer vision models don't.

Abstract

Large-scale foundation models (FMs) in remote sensing (RS) are developed following paradigms established in computer vision (CV), yet the validity of transferring CV scaling laws to RS has not been systematically examined. We hypothesize that RS FMs enter an overparameterized regime at substantially smaller scales than their CV counterparts, with task-relevant information encoded redundantly across model dimensions.

To test this hypothesis, we apply post-hoc slimmability, uniform width reduction of pretrained encoder transformer blocks, as a tool to measure representational redundancy across eight state-of-the-art RS FMs on classification, segmentation, and change detection tasks. RS FMs retain 69–109% relative accuracy on RS datasets under aggressive width reduction, while MAE and DINOv2 pretrained on natural images degrade sharply on ImageNet subsets of matched class count over the same range of computational requirements. A CV MAE evaluated directly on the same RS datasets narrows but does not close the gap, indicating that both dataset characteristics and domain-specific pretraining contribute.

Mechanistic analyses such as feature correlation, explained variance, and effective dimensionality indicate that task-relevant variance concentrates in few principal components and is redundantly encoded across model dimensions. We further show that learned slimmable training improves over post-hoc slimmability for contrastive objectives, while reconstruction-based objectives do not benefit from current slimmable training protocols. Our findings establish post-hoc slimming as a practical deployment strategy for resource-constrained RS applications and as a diagnostic tool for representational redundancy in RS FMs.

Key findings

RS FMs are highly slimmable. At their smallest compute budget, most models keep more than 80% of their full-scale accuracy on classification. Segmentation and change detection are even more robust.

The gap to CV is real, not an artefact of class count. CV MAE and DINOv2 collapse on ImageNet subsets with the same number of classes. On the same RS data, a CV MAE still degrades faster than RS FMs.

Smaller can be better. Intermediate widths often match or beat the full model, consistent with redundant dimensions acting as noise for the task.

Learned slimming helps contrastive, not reconstructive, pretraining. Slimmable MoCo improves at low budgets; slimmable MAE does not benefit under current protocols.

Redundancy is visible in the features. Slimmed representations concentrate variance in few, highly correlated directions, and this suffices for the downstream task.

Method

Post-hoc slimming: one knob, no retraining

We take a released checkpoint and keep only a fraction s of the hidden width in every block: the first ⌊s·dk⌋ dimensions of each attention head and the first ⌊s·dh⌋ units of each feed-forward layer. The method is deliberately simple. If a sophisticated compression method preserved accuracy, it would be unclear whether that reflects the method or the model. If naive slimming preserves accuracy, it tells us something about the model.

with \(d_h' = \max(1, \lfloor s\cdot d_h \rfloor)\), \(s\in(0,1]\).

Slimmable MHSA $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{I}&=\textstyle\bigcup_{h=1}^{H}\,[h d_k,\,h d_k + d_k')\\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{attn}}&=\mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d_k'}\,\big)\mathbf{V}\end{aligned}$$

\(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\) use rows \(\mathcal{I}\) of their projections, the output projection uses columns \(\mathcal{I}\). All \(H\) heads are kept.

Evaluation

Frozen features, 31 widths

k-NN (k = 5) or linear probe on penultimate features for classification; a SegFormer-style multi-scale head for segmentation and change detection. Five seeds for classification; one run per width for segmentation and change detection.

Metrics

Relative compute and retention

Compute = FLOPs at width s / FLOPs at full width. Retention = metric(s) / metric(1.0), so datasets with different absolute scores are comparable.

Datasets

GEO-Bench + OSCD

Learned vs. post-hoc

Does training for slimmability help?

We pretrain ViT-Base models from scratch with MoCo and with MAE, once regularly and once with slimmable training (full, minimal and three random widths per step, with distillation from the full network). Then we slim all four post hoc.

Representational analysis

Why does slimming work?

Three descriptive measures of the frozen features at each width. They give mechanistic intuition, not a causal proof.

Mean feature correlation

Average absolute correlation between feature dimensions, over the strictly upper triangle of the correlation matrix (self-correlations excluded).

$$\mathrm{MFC}=\tfrac{2}{D(D-1)}\textstyle\sum_{i<j}\lvert\mathrm{corr}(\mathbf{e}_i,\mathbf{e}_j)\rvert$$

High = dimensions are redundant copies of each other.

Explained variance ratio

Share of variance per principal component of the mean-centred features. We report the slope of log EVR against log component index.

$$\mathrm{EVR}_i=\lambda_i\big/\textstyle\sum_j\lambda_j$$

Steeper (more negative) = variance concentrated in fewer components.

Effective dimensionality

Participation ratio of the covariance eigenvalues: how many dimensions carry independent variance.

$$d_{\text{eff}}=\big(\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i\big)^2\big/\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i^2$$

Low = information lives in a few directions.

DOFA needs many independent dimensions at full scale (up to 34 on m-bigearthnet) and slimming removes them. Prithvi keeps 90–100% retention while its effective dimension varies by more than 2.5×.

DOFA (large): monotonic
All three measures change monotonically with width: correlation drops to near zero, the EVR slope rises from ≈ −3.1 to ≈ −1.1, and effective dimension grows to 34. Retention tracks effective dimension closely, so this model needs many independent dimensions at full scale.
TerraMind-1.0 (large): saturating
Effective dimension rises from ≈3.6 to ≈5.7 by 40% compute and then plateaus; the EVR slope is flat over most of the range. Once a moderate budget is reached, the model keeps a consistent representational strategy.
Prithvi-EO-2.0 (600M): decoupled
Correlation and EVR slope are non-monotonic, and effective dimension peaks around 40–50% compute before dropping sharply, while retention stays within 90–100% throughout. We considered incomplete pretraining convergence as an explanation, but could not verify it (no loss curves were available), so we leave it as an explicitly untested hypothesis.
Beyond the submission

New analyses in the camera-ready

Experiments added during the review process. They test whether the redundancy is an artefact of frozen features, model selection, geography or class balance.

Fine-tuning
Full fine-tuning (LR 3e-3, three seeds) raises absolute performance, but the accuracy-vs-width curves keep their shape. The redundancy is not an artefact of the frozen-feature protocol.

Why might RS FMs differ?

Three plausible, non-exclusive explanations. We did not test them directly.

Lower-level features suffice
RS models rely mostly on low- and mid-level features (spectral signatures, textures, local layouts), while CV models rely more on high-level semantics such as global shape. If a task is solvable from lower-level cues, extra capacity mostly adds redundancy.
Fewer nuisance factors
Satellite images are acquired under fairly standardised viewing geometries and sensors. Natural images vary far more in viewpoint, pose, illumination and occlusion. Learning fewer invariances may need less capacity.
Benchmarks may be too easy
Current RS benchmarks may not stress modern FMs. Class count alone does not explain the gap (CV models stay less slimmable on ImageNet subsets with matched class count), but harder temporal, multi-modal or geospatial reasoning tasks could show larger benefits from scale.

Scope and limitations

  • Findings describe post-hoc width redundancy of the evaluated checkpoints, not a general scaling law for RS FMs.
  • Read relative retention together with absolute performance, especially for safety-critical uses.
  • Redundancy shrinks under geographic distribution shift, and aggregate metrics can mask class-wise degradation.
  • All models are ViT-based and pretrained on optical Sentinel-2 data. SAR, CNN, hybrid, state-space and generative models (e.g. DiffusionSat) remain open.
  • Complementary to scaling studies that pretrain one model per size (e.g. Dionelis et al., 2025): we ask how much of an already trained model is load-bearing.

Code & reproducibility

Open code, pinned environment

All code for the experiments on this page will be released.

  • MIT license.
  • Pinned dependencies via Poetry's lockfile, plus the resolved environment specification.
  • Checkpoint sources for every evaluated model.
  • Fixed seeds: experiments with n runs use seeds {0, …, n−1}.
  • CPU-friendly analysis: k-NN, EVR, correlation and effective dimension run on 16 CPU cores; feature extraction for all widths takes under 30 minutes per model.
# Illustration of the idea, not the released API
def slim_ffn(x, fc1, fc2, s):
    dh = max(1, int(s * fc1.out_features))
    h = act(x @ fc1.weight[:dh].T + fc1.bias[:dh])
    return h @ fc2.weight[:, :dh].T + fc2.bias

def slim_heads(W, b, H, dk, s):
    k = max(1, int(s * dk))
    idx = torch.cat([torch.arange(i*dk, i*dk + k) for i in range(H)])
    return W[idx], b[idx]   # rows for Q, K, V; columns for W_O
